## 1.1 Before you read: how to use this book

Read each section slowly, **out loud**. German spelling is much more regular than English spelling: once you know the rules in section 1.4, you can pronounce almost every word correctly the first time you see it. That is why this book starts with sounds before grammar.

Every German sentence has a speaker button, and a small **slow** button next to it that plays it at a slower pace. At the top of the page you can choose a male or a female voice and switch the English translations on or off. A good habit: **cover the translation, read the German, then check**.

At the end of each section you will find exercises. They tell you why an answer is right or wrong, so do not skip the explanations. They are part of the lesson.

## 1.2 A first meeting

Anna and Raj meet on the first day of a German course in Berlin. Read the dialogue once for meaning, then again aloud.

```dialogue
setting: Anna and Raj meet at the entrance of the language school.
---
Anna: Hallo! Ich heiße Anna. Und du? | Hi! My name is Anna. And you?
Raj: Hallo, Anna. Ich heiße Raj. | Hi, Anna. My name is Raj.
Anna: Woher kommst du, Raj? | Where do you come from, Raj?
Raj: Ich komme aus Indien. Und du? | I come from India. And you?
Anna: Ich komme aus Deutschland. Wie geht's? | I come from Germany. How are you?
Raj: Gut, danke. Und dir? | Fine, thanks. And you?
Anna: Auch gut. Tschüss, Raj! | Also fine. Bye, Raj!
Raj: Tschüss, Anna! Bis morgen! | Bye, Anna! See you tomorrow!
```

Three things to notice before we go on. First, **there is no word for "am" in "my name is"**: German says *Ich heiße Anna*, literally "I am-called Anna". Second, *du* is used between two students: it is the informal "you". Third, *Wie geht's?* is short for *Wie geht es dir?*, literally "How goes it to you?". You will see why it is *dir* and not *du* when we meet the dative case later in the book.

## 1.3 Saying hello and goodbye

German greetings depend on **the time of day** and on **how formal** the situation is.

| German | Meaning | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Guten Morgen | good morning | until about 10 or 11 o'clock |
| Guten Tag | hello (literally "good day") | during the day; polite and neutral |
| Guten Abend | good evening | from the late afternoon on |
| Gute Nacht | good night | only when someone is going to bed |
| Hallo | hello, hi | any time, informal |
| Tschüss | bye | informal, very common |
| Auf Wiedersehen | goodbye | formal; literally "until we see each other again" |
| Bis morgen | see you tomorrow | informal; *bis* means "until" |

```merke
**Gute Nacht is not "good evening".** It means goodnight and is only said when parting for the night. If you meet someone at 8 p.m., say *Guten Abend*.
```

```achtung
In writing, the greeting is capitalised: *Guten Morgen*. Greeting words are **not** a place where English helps you: there is no German word for "good afternoon". Use *Guten Tag* until the evening.
```

Regional greetings are worth recognising even if you do not use them yet. In northern Germany you will hear *Moin* at any time of day. In Bavaria and Austria people say *Servus* and *Grüß Gott*, and in Switzerland *Grüezi*. All of them mean "hello".

```examples
Guten Morgen, Frau Weber! | Good morning, Mrs Weber!
Guten Tag, Herr Patel. | Good day, Mr Patel.
Guten Abend! | Good evening!
Gute Nacht, Anna! | Good night, Anna!
Hallo, wie geht's? | Hi, how are you?
Auf Wiedersehen! | Goodbye!
Tschüss, bis morgen! | Bye, see you tomorrow!
```

### Bitte, danke, Entschuldigung

Three little words do a lot of work in everyday life.

- **danke** means "thank you". The polite reply is **bitte**.
- **bitte** has two jobs: it means "please" when you ask for something, and "you're welcome" when you answer *danke*. English needs two words; German uses one.
- **Entschuldigung** means "excuse me" (to get attention) and "sorry" (for a small mistake). For something more serious, say *Es tut mir leid*, "I'm sorry".

```examples
Einen Kaffee, bitte. | A coffee, please.
Danke! – Bitte! | Thank you! – You're welcome!
Entschuldigung, wie bitte? | Excuse me, pardon?
Ja, bitte. | Yes, please.
Nein, danke. | No, thank you.
```

```tipp
When you do not understand something, say **Wie bitte?** ("Pardon?"). It is polite and every German speaker understands it immediately.
```

## 1.4 The sounds of German

German is spelled much more consistently than English. Learn the rules below once and you can read aloud almost anything, even words you do not understand yet. Keep in mind that the English comparisons are approximations: always listen to the 🔊 buttons too.

### The alphabet

German uses the same 26 letters as English, plus **ä, ö, ü** (called *Umlaute*) and **ß** (called *Eszett* or "scharfes S").

To spell a word aloud, say the letter names in a row. On the phone you will also hear spelling words ("A wie Anton"), but the plain letter names are enough for now.

### Vowels: long or short

Every vowel has a long and a short version. The rule of thumb that works most of the time:

- A vowel is **long** before **one** consonant, before **h**, or when it is doubled: *Name* /ˈnaːmə/, *Sohn* /zoːn/, *Tee* /teː/.
- A vowel is **short** before **two or more** consonants: *Land* /lant/, *kommen* /ˈkɔmən/, *Mutter* /ˈmʊtɐ/.

The **h** after a vowel is silent. It only tells you the vowel is long: *Zahl* /tsaːl/, *Uhr* /uːɐ̯/.

| Vowel | long | short |
|---|---|---|
| a | *Name* "ah" (as in "father") | *Land* (a short "ah", like "u" in "cup" but clearer) |
| e | *Tee* like "ay" in "say", without the y | *Bett* like "e" in "bet" |
| i | *Kino* like "ee" in "see" | *bitte* like "i" in "sit" |
| o | *Brot* like "oh" in "go", without the w | *kommen* like "o" in "pot" |
| u | *gut* like "oo" in "moon" | *Mutter* like "u" in "put" |

An unstressed **e** at the end of a word is a soft "uh", as in the "a" of "about": *Name* /ˈnaːmə/, *bitte* /ˈbɪtə/.

### The umlauts ä, ö, ü

These three sounds do not exist in English, so they are worth practising.

- **ä** is almost the same as short **e**: *Männer*, *Käse*. When it is long it sounds like the "e" in "pet" held longer: *Käse* /ˈkɛːzə/.
- **ö**: say "eh" and round your lips as if saying "oh". It sounds a bit like the vowel in "bird" with no r: *schön* /ʃøːn/, *Köln*.
- **ü**: say "ee" and round your lips as if saying "oo". *für* /fyːɐ̯/, *Tschüss* /tʃʏs/, *grün*.

If your keyboard cannot type them, write **ae, oe, ue** instead (*schoen*, *Tschuess*) and **ss** for ß. Native speakers do exactly this in emails and passwords.

### The letter ß

**ß** is simply a double s. It appears after long vowels and diphthongs: *heißen* /ˈhaɪ̯sn̩/, *Fuß*, *groß*. After a short vowel you write **ss**: *Wasser*, *muss*. There is no capital ß in everyday writing.

### Diphthongs

Four vowel pairs are single sounds and are the source of many beginner errors. **Learn them by the second letter in the pair:**

| Spelling | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ei** (also ai) | "eye" | *nein*, *zwei*, *heißen* |
| **ie** | long "ee" | *wie*, *vier*, *sieben* |
| **eu** (also äu) | "oy" in "boy" | *neun*, *Deutsch*, *Häuser* |
| **au** | "ow" in "cow" | *Frau*, *auf*, *aus* |

```achtung
**ei and ie are mirror images and sound completely different.** *ei* is "eye": *nein* means "no" and sounds like "nine". *ie* is "ee": *Wie* sounds like "vee". Many learners mix up *Wein* (wine) and *wie* (how). The *second* letter tells you the sound.
```

### Consonants that surprise English speakers

| Spelling | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **w** | like English **v** | *Wasser* /ˈvasɐ/, *wie* |
| **v** | usually like English **f** | *Vater* /ˈfaːtɐ/, *viel* |
| **z** | always **ts** | *Zahl* /tsaːl/, *zwei* |
| **j** | like English **y** | *Ja* /jaː/, *Jahr* |
| **s** before a vowel | like English **z** | *sieben* /ˈziːbn̩/, *Sonne* |
| **sch** | like English **sh** | *Tschüss*, *schön* |
| **sp, st** at the start of a word | **shp, sht** | *sprechen* /ˈʃpʁɛçn̩/, *Straße* |
| **ch** after e, i, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu | soft, like a hissing "h" in "huge" | *ich*, *Milch*, *nicht* |
| **ch** after a, o, u, au | harsh, from the back of the throat, like the Scottish "loch" | *Buch*, *nach*, *auch* |
| **r** | usually a soft throaty sound; after a vowel at the end of a word it is a faint "uh" | *rot*, *Bruder*, *Vater* |
| **qu** | **kv** | *Quiz* |

The two sounds of **ch** are really one letter pair with two neighbours: the *ich*-sound after "front" vowels and the *ach*-sound after "back" vowels. A good test is the pair **ich / ach**: say them one after another and feel where your tongue is.

```merke
**Final devoicing.** At the end of a word, **b, d, g** are pronounced as **p, t, k**: *Tag* /taːk/, *und* /ʊnt/, *Land* /lant/, *ab* /ap/. In the plural the sound returns: *Tage* /ˈtaːɡə/.
```

### Where is the stress?

Almost every German word is stressed on the **first syllable**: *Ma*-ma, *Na*-me, *Ta*-ge. There are two common exceptions you will meet early: words that begin with **be-, ge-, ver-, er-** put the stress on the *next* syllable (*be*-**zah**-len, *Ge*-**mü**-se), and many words borrowed from French or Latin are stressed late (*Stu*-**dent**, *Tele*-**fon**).

## 1.5 Counting: 0 to 20

Numbers up to twelve are individual words. From thirteen on, the pattern is regular: **units + zehn** (thirteen = three-ten).

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 **null** | 1 **eins** | 2 **zwei** | 3 **drei** |
| 4 **vier** | 5 **fünf** | 6 **sechs** | 7 **sieben** |
| 8 **acht** | 9 **neun** | 10 **zehn** | 11 **elf** |
| 12 **zwölf** | 13 **dreizehn** | 14 **vierzehn** | 15 **fünfzehn** |
| 16 **sechzehn** | 17 **siebzehn** | 18 **achtzehn** | 19 **neunzehn** |
| 20 **zwanzig** | | | |

Note two small spelling changes: **sechs → sechzehn** (the "s" is dropped) and **sieben → siebzehn** (the "ben" is dropped).

```tipp
On the phone, **zwei** is often said **zwo** so that it is not mixed up with **drei**. When someone dictates a phone number, you will hear *zwo* a lot.
```

```examples
Meine Telefonnummer ist null eins sieben sechs. | My phone number is zero one seven six.
Zwei Kaffee, bitte. | Two coffees, please.
Das macht zwölf Euro. | That makes twelve euros.
Ich bin fünfzehn. | I am fifteen.
```

```kultur
Germans usually say phone numbers as single digits or in pairs. Prices are said like numbers: *elf Euro fünfzig* is 11.50. Note that the decimal separator in German is a **comma**: 11,50 €.
```

## 1.6 Wie geht's? Small talk

After the greeting comes the question *How are you?*. German has an informal and a formal version, and the choice depends on the same *du* / *Sie* rule you will meet in chapter 2.

| | question | answer |
|---|---|---|
| informal | Wie geht's? / Wie geht es dir? | Gut, danke. Und dir? |
| formal | Wie geht es Ihnen? | Danke, gut. Und Ihnen? |

Useful answers, from good to not so good:

```examples
Sehr gut, danke! | Very well, thank you!
Gut, danke. | Fine, thanks.
Es geht. | It's OK (so-so).
Nicht so gut. | Not so good.
Schlecht. | Bad.
Ich bin müde. | I am tired.
```

```englisch
**Do not translate "How are you?" word for word.** In English, "How are you?" is often just a greeting. In German, *Wie geht es dir?* is a real question and Germans often answer honestly. Between close friends, a short answer is fine: *Gut, und dir?*
```

## 1.7 Summary

```merke
- German is spelled regularly. **ei = "eye"**, **ie = "ee"**, **eu = "oy"**, **au = "ow"**, **w = v**, **v = f**, **z = ts**, **j = y**, **sch = sh**, **sp/st = shp/sht** at the start of a word.
- A vowel before **one** consonant is long, before **two** it is short. A silent **h** marks length.
- Greet by time of day: **Guten Morgen, Guten Tag, Guten Abend**. *Gute Nacht* is only for going to bed.
- **Hallo** and **Tschüss** are informal. **Guten Tag** and **Auf Wiedersehen** are formal or neutral.
- **bitte** = please / you're welcome. **danke** = thank you. **Entschuldigung** = excuse me / sorry.
- Numbers 13–19 are **units + zehn**, with **sechzehn** and **siebzehn** shortened.
- **Wie geht's?** is informal. **Wie geht es Ihnen?** is formal.
```

```kultur
**Landeskunde: where is German spoken?** German is the official language of Germany, Austria and Liechtenstein, and one of four official languages in Switzerland (alongside French, Italian and Romansh). It is also spoken in parts of Italy (South Tyrol), Belgium and Luxembourg. Each region has its own greetings and accent, but the written standard you are learning is the same everywhere.
```
