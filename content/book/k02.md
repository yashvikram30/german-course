## 2.1 Two conversations

The same question, *Wie heißen Sie?* or *Wie heißt du?*, can sound very different depending on who you are talking to. Read both dialogues aloud, then notice how every verb changes.

```dialogue
title: Formal: Frau Weber and Herr Patel
setting: Frau Weber is a teacher. Raj Patel arrives for his first lesson. They have never met.
---
Frau Weber: Guten Tag! Wie heißen Sie? | Good day! What is your name?
Herr Patel: Guten Tag. Ich heiße Raj Patel. | Good day. My name is Raj Patel.
Frau Weber: Woher kommen Sie, Herr Patel? | Where do you come from, Mr Patel?
Herr Patel: Ich komme aus Indien. Und Sie? | I come from India. And you?
Frau Weber: Ich komme aus Österreich, aber ich wohne in Berlin. | I come from Austria, but I live in Berlin.
Herr Patel: Sprechen Sie Hindi? | Do you speak Hindi?
Frau Weber: Nein, aber ich spreche Englisch und Deutsch. | No, but I speak English and German.
```

```dialogue
title: Informal: two students
setting: Anna and Raj sit next to each other in class.
---
Anna: Wie heißt du? | What is your name?
Raj: Ich heiße Raj. Und du? | My name is Raj. And you?
Anna: Ich heiße Anna. Wo wohnst du? | My name is Anna. Where do you live?
Raj: Ich wohne in Berlin. Und du? | I live in Berlin. And you?
Anna: Ich auch. Wie alt bist du? | Me too. How old are you?
Raj: Ich bin vierundzwanzig. Und du? | I'm twenty-four. And you?
Anna: Ich bin zweiundzwanzig. | I'm twenty-two.
```

What changed between the two conversations? **Sie** with *-en* verbs (*heißen Sie, kommen Sie, sprechen Sie*) versus **du** with *-st* verbs (*heißt du, wohnst du*). The rest of the chapter explains why, one piece at a time.

## 2.2 Personal pronouns: who is doing it?

A **pronoun** replaces a noun. If you say *Anna wohnt in Berlin. Sie ist Studentin.*, the word *sie* replaces *Anna*. German chooses the pronoun by **person** (first, second, third), **number** (one or more) and, for the third person, **gender**.

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| 1st (speaker) | **ich** (I) | **wir** (we) |
| 2nd, informal | **du** (you) | **ihr** (you, several people) |
| 2nd, formal | **Sie** (you) | **Sie** (you) |
| 3rd | **er** (he) · **sie** (she) · **es** (it) | **sie** (they) |

Four points are worth a closer look.

1. **ich is not capitalised** unless it begins a sentence. *Du* and *ihr* are written with small letters in normal text.
2. **German has two kinds of "you" for one person** (*du* and *Sie*) and one extra for several people (*ihr*). English *you* covers all of them.
3. **Sie (formal "you") is always written with a capital S**, in every position in the sentence. That is the main way to tell it apart from *sie* (she, they) on the page.
4. **sie** has three meanings: *she*, *they* and (capitalised) *Sie* = formal *you*. In speech they sound identical. The **verb ending** tells you which one it is:

```examples
Sie kommt aus Indien. | She comes from India.
Sie kommen aus Indien. | They come from India. / You (formal) come from India.
Kommen Sie aus Indien? | Do you (formal) come from India?
```

```merke
**Grammatical gender and natural gender are different things.** *er, sie, es* replace nouns by their grammatical gender, not only by the sex of a person. A table is *der Tisch*, so you say *er* for it. You will learn how to find a noun's gender in chapter 3. For now, use *er* for a man, *sie* for a woman and *es* for a child or a thing like *das Auto* ("it").
```

## 2.3 du or Sie?

This is the first real cultural decision a learner has to make, and it is easier than it sounds.

- Use **du** with friends, family, children, pets, fellow students and, in many workplaces and clubs, colleagues. Online, *du* is the standard.
- Use **Sie** with strangers, officials, shop staff, your teacher, your boss and anyone older than you whom you do not know well.
- **If in doubt, use Sie.** Nobody will mind that you are too polite. Being too casual to a stranger can sound rude.
- The switch to *du* is usually offered by the older or more senior person: *Wollen wir uns duzen?* ("Shall we use du?"). It is also fine to ask.
- Sie goes with **Herr/Frau + surname**: *Frau Weber, Herr Patel*. Du goes with first names.

```kultur
Moving from *Sie* to *du* is a small social event in German-speaking countries. People sometimes celebrate it with a drink: *das Du anbieten*, "to offer the du". Don't be surprised if a colleague uses *du* from day one: many start-ups, universities and Scandinavian-style companies do. When you meet someone in a shop, at the post office or on the phone for the first time, *Sie* is the safe choice.
```

## 2.4 Verbs: the infinitive, the stem and the endings

A verb in a dictionary appears in the **infinitive**, the "to…" form. Almost all German infinitives end in **-en**: *kommen* (to come), *wohnen* (to live), *heißen* (to be called), *lernen* (to learn). To use the verb in a sentence you **remove -en** to get the **stem** and add an **ending** that matches the subject.

| | Ending | kommen | wohnen | heißen | lernen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ich | **-e** | komm**e** | wohn**e** | heiß**e** | lern**e** |
| du | **-st** | komm**st** | wohn**st** | heiß**t** | lern**st** |
| er / sie / es | **-t** | komm**t** | wohn**t** | heiß**t** | lern**t** |
| wir | **-en** | komm**en** | wohn**en** | heiß**en** | lern**en** |
| ihr | **-t** | komm**t** | wohn**t** | heiß**t** | lern**t** |
| sie (they) | **-en** | komm**en** | wohn**en** | heiß**en** | lern**en** |
| Sie (formal) | **-en** | komm**en** | wohn**en** | heiß**en** | lern**en** |

Look at the patterns, they make the table easy to learn:

- **wir, sie (they) and Sie always use the infinitive form.** Three of the seven forms need no thinking at all.
- **er/sie/es and ihr both end in -t.**
- **ich is the only form that ends in -e.**

```achtung
**Don't write "ich kommen".** English speakers often keep the infinitive for every person. German needs the ending: *ich komme, du kommst, er kommt*.
```

A German present-tense verb covers several English forms: **Ich wohne in Berlin** means "I live in Berlin" and "I am living in Berlin". There is no "do" in questions or negatives: *Wohnst du in Berlin?* (not *Do you live…*).

### Small spelling adjustments

Most verbs follow the table exactly. Three groups need a small change to stay pronounceable:

| Situation | What changes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Stem ends in **-t** or **-d** | an **-e-** is added before **-st** and **-t** | arbeiten → du arbeit**e**st, er arbeit**e**t; finden → du find**e**st |
| Stem ends in an **s-sound** (-s, -ß, -z, -x) | the **-st** of *du* becomes **-t** | heißen → du heiß**t**; tanzen → du tanz**t** |
| Infinitive ends in **-eln / -ern** | **-e** of the stem is dropped, *ich* keeps **-e** | wandern → ich wandere, wir wandern |

## 2.5 The verb sein ("to be")

**sein** is the most important German verb, and it is irregular: its forms do not follow the stem + ending pattern. You must simply learn them by heart, and you will use them every day.

| | sein | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ich | **bin** | Ich bin Raj. |
| du | **bist** | Du bist müde. |
| er / sie / es | **ist** | Sie ist aus Deutschland. |
| wir | **sind** | Wir sind Studenten. |
| ihr | **seid** | Ihr seid sehr nett. |
| sie (they) | **sind** | Sie sind aus Indien. |
| Sie (formal) | **sind** | Sind Sie Herr Patel? |

Use *sein* for **who** or **what** someone is, for origin, for descriptions and for age:

```examples
Ich bin Student. | I am a student.
Raj ist nicht müde. | Raj is not tired.
Wir sind aus Deutschland. | We are from Germany.
Ich bin vierundzwanzig (Jahre alt). | I am twenty-four (years old).
Wie alt bist du? | How old are you?
```

```merke
**No article before a profession.** German says *Ich bin Student* (not "ein Student") and *Sie ist Lehrerin*. Female forms usually add **-in**: *der Student → die Studentin, der Lehrer → die Lehrerin*.
```

## 2.6 Asking questions

German has two kinds of questions. The word order is different for each.

### Questions with a question word (W-questions)

Question words start with **w-**, so they are easy to remember. The verb comes **immediately after** the question word, in second position.

| Question word | Means | Typical answer |
|---|---|---|
| **wer** | who | Das ist Anna. |
| **was** | what | Ich bin Student. |
| **wie** | how | Ich heiße Raj. · Gut, danke. |
| **woher** | from where | Ich komme aus Indien. |
| **wo** | where | Ich wohne in Berlin. |

```examples
Wie heißt du? | What is your name?
Woher kommen Sie? | Where do you come from? (formal)
Wo wohnst du? | Where do you live?
Wer ist das? | Who is that?
Wie alt sind Sie? | How old are you? (formal)
```

```englisch
English *how* and German *wie* look alike but are not always the same: you ask for someone's name with **wie** (*Wie heißt du?*), literally "How are you called?". Don't say *Was ist dein Name?* when you can say *Wie heißt du?*; both are correct, but the second is much more natural.
```

### Yes/no questions

If the answer is *ja* or *nein*, the **verb comes first**. There is no extra word like English "do".

```examples
Kommst du aus Indien? | Do you come from India?
Wohnen Sie in Berlin? | Do you live in Berlin?
Sind Sie Herr Patel? | Are you Mr Patel?
Ist Anna müde? | Is Anna tired?
```

You can answer in full or briefly: *Ja, ich komme aus Indien.* or just *Ja.* If the answer is no: *Nein, ich komme aus Deutschland.*

## 2.7 Word order in statements

In a normal German statement the **conjugated verb is always in second position**. That means **second position in the sentence**, not necessarily the second word. The first position can hold the subject or something else. When something other than the subject comes first, the subject moves behind the verb.

| 1st position | verb (2nd) | rest |
|---|---|---|
| Ich | wohne | in Berlin. |
| In Berlin | wohne | ich. |
| Morgen | arbeite | ich nicht. |
| Heute | bin | ich müde. |

Both *Ich wohne in Berlin* and *In Berlin wohne ich* mean the same thing. The second is slightly more emphatic: "It's in Berlin that I live."

```merke
**Verb second.** In statements and in W-questions the conjugated verb stands in position 2. In yes/no questions it stands in position 1.
```

### Saying "not": nicht

*Nicht* negates the verb or the whole statement and normally goes at the **end**:

```examples
Ich komme nicht aus Indien. | I'm not from India.
Ich arbeite heute nicht. | I'm not working today.
Anna ist nicht müde. | Anna is not tired.
```

When you simply contradict a statement, you can also answer with *nein*: *Kommst du aus China? – Nein, ich komme nicht aus China.* The word **kein** ("no / not a") is introduced in chapter 3.

## 2.8 Countries, cities, languages and age

### Where I am from, where I live, what I speak

Three small patterns give you a lot of conversational power:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **kommen aus** + country/city | *Ich komme aus Indien. Ich komme aus Hamburg.* |
| **wohnen in** + city/country | *Ich wohne in Berlin. Wir wohnen in Deutschland.* |
| **sprechen** + language | *Ich spreche Englisch und Hindi.* |

Most countries have **no article**: *Deutschland, Österreich, Indien, Italien*. A few do, and they are the exceptions to learn: **die Schweiz, die Türkei, die USA**. After *aus* and *in* they need the dative case, which you will learn properly later in the book; for now just copy the whole phrase: *aus der Schweiz, aus den USA, in der Schweiz, in den USA*.

```examples
Ich komme aus Österreich. | I come from Austria.
Sie kommt aus der Schweiz. | She comes from Switzerland.
Wir wohnen in den USA. | We live in the USA.
Er spricht Spanisch und Französisch. | He speaks Spanish and French.
```

The verb **sprechen** is *irregular*: the stem vowel changes from **e** to **i** for *du* and *er/sie/es*: **ich spreche, du sprichst, er/sie spricht**. A group of other common verbs (essen, lesen, sehen, geben) do the same, and a later chapter collects them.

### Numbers from 21 to 100

German reverses the order you know from English: **units first, then tens**, joined by **und** and written as **one word**: 21 = *einundzwanzig* ("one-and-twenty"), 34 = *vierunddreißig*, 99 = *neunundneunzig*.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 20 **zwanzig** | 30 **dreißig** | 40 **vierzig** |
| 50 **fünfzig** | 60 **sechzig** | 70 **siebzig** |
| 80 **achtzig** | 90 **neunzig** | 100 **hundert** |

Watch for **dreißig** (the only ten that ends in -ßig), **sechzig** and **siebzig** (like sechzehn and siebzehn, the "s" and "ben" are shortened), and the number **eins**, which becomes **ein-** in compounds: *einundzwanzig* (not *einsundzwanzig*).

```examples
Ich bin einundzwanzig. | I am twenty-one.
Wie alt bist du? – Ich bin sechsunddreißig. | How old are you? – I am thirty-six.
Hundert Euro. | A hundred euros.
```

## 2.10 Summary

```merke
- **Pronouns:** ich, du, er/sie/es, wir, ihr, sie, **Sie** (formal). Use **du** with friends and children, **Sie** with strangers and in formal situations.
- **Regular verbs:** stem + **-e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en**. wir, sie and Sie use the infinitive.
- **Adjustments:** stems in -t/-d add -e- (*du arbeitest*); s-sounds drop the s of -st (*du heißt*).
- **sein:** bin, bist, ist, sind, seid, sind, sind.
- **sprechen:** ich spreche, du **sprichst**, er **spricht**.
- **Questions:** W-word + verb (*Wo wohnst du?*). Yes/no: verb first (*Wohnst du in Berlin?*).
- **Verb second** in statements. **nicht** negates.
- **Countries:** *aus Indien, in Deutschland*. A few have articles: *die Schweiz, die Türkei, die USA*.
- **Numbers 21–99:** units + **und** + tens, one word.
```

```kultur
**Landeskunde: names and titles.** Germans introduce themselves with their surname when formal: *Mein Name ist Weber*. In offices, *Herr Weber* and *Frau Weber* are the norm. Titles like *Doktor* or *Professor* are part of the name: *Frau Doktor Weber*. In everyday life first names are used as soon as *du* is agreed.
```
