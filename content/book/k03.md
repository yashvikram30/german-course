## 3.1 A family photo

Anna shows Raj a photo on her phone. Listen for the little words in front of the nouns: *ein, eine, der, die, das, mein, meine*. They are the subject of this chapter.

```dialogue
setting: Anna and Raj look at a photo of her family after class.
---
Raj: Wer ist das? | Who is that?
Anna: Das ist meine Familie. Das ist mein Vater und das ist meine Mutter. | That's my family. That's my father and that's my mother.
Raj: Hast du Geschwister? | Do you have siblings?
Anna: Ja, ich habe einen Bruder und eine Schwester. Mein Bruder heißt Jonas. | Yes, I have a brother and a sister. My brother is called Jonas.
Raj: Und das ist ein Hund? | And that's a dog?
Anna: Nein, das ist kein Hund, das ist eine Katze! Sie heißt Mimi. | No, that's not a dog, that's a cat! Her name is Mimi.
Raj: Wie schön. Ich habe keine Katze, aber ich habe einen Hund. | How nice. I don't have a cat, but I have a dog.
```

## 3.2 Nouns: capital letters and genders

**Every noun is written with a capital letter**, wherever it appears in the sentence: *der Tisch, die Lampe, das Buch, Ich habe einen Bruder.* This is a gift for the reader: you can see the nouns at a glance.

Every noun also has a **grammatical gender**: masculine, feminine or neuter. The gender is shown by the **definite article**, the German "the":

| Gender | Article (the) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | **der** | der Tisch, der Mann, der Vater |
| feminine | **die** | die Lampe, die Frau, die Mutter |
| neuter | **das** | das Buch, das Kind, das Haus |
| plural (all genders) | **die** | die Tische, die Lampen, die Bücher |

Gender is a property of the *word*, not of the thing. A table is not masculine in any real sense; the word *Tisch* just happens to be masculine, and *das Mädchen* ("the girl") is neuter. English speakers find this strange, but there is a payoff: once you know a noun's gender, the rest of the sentence follows from it.

### The golden rule for learning nouns

```merke
**Always learn a noun together with its article and plural**: *der Tisch, -e*; *die Lampe, -n*; *das Buch, ¨-er*. Never learn "Tisch" on its own. This book writes every noun that way and colours the article: **der** blue, **die** red, **das** green.
```

### Rules of thumb for gender

For many nouns there is no rule and you must memorise the article. But a surprising number follow patterns. These rules are very reliable:

| Rule | Gender | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **People and animals** follow their natural sex | der / die | der Vater, die Mutter, der Bruder, die Schwester, der Mann, die Frau |
| **Compound nouns** take the gender of the **last** part | | der Kühlschrank (der Schrank), die Freizeit (die Zeit), das Handy**foto** (das Foto) |
| **-ung, -heit, -keit** | **die** | die Wohnung (flat), die Einladung (invitation), die Möglichkeit (possibility) |
| **-in** (female form of a person) | **die** | die Lehrerin, die Freundin, die Studentin |
| **-er** (a male doer) | **der** | der Lehrer, der Fahrer (driver) |
| **-chen, -lein** (diminutives) | **das** | das Mädchen (girl), das Brötchen (bread roll) |
| most nouns ending in **-e** | **die** | die Lampe, die Katze, die Tasche, die Schule |
| most nouns ending in **-o** | **das** | das Auto, das Foto, das Kino |

Several exceptions are worth knowing from the start: *das Kind* and *das Baby* (young people and animals are neuter), *der Kaffee, der Tee, der Name* (they look like "-e" nouns but are masculine).

If no rule applies, the safest guess is **der**, because masculine nouns are slightly more frequent. It is only a guess, so check the glossary.

## 3.3 ein and eine: "a" and "an"

The **indefinite article** is German "a / an". It also shows gender:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|---|---|---|---|
| definite (the) | **der** Tisch | **die** Lampe | **das** Buch |
| indefinite (a) | **ein** Tisch | **eine** Lampe | **ein** Buch |

Notice that **masculine and neuter both use *ein***. Only the feminine adds **-e**: *eine*. There is **no plural of ein**: *ein Tisch* becomes just *Tische* ("tables"), without an article.

The basic question-and-answer pair for everything around you is:

```examples
Was ist das? – Das ist ein Tisch. | What is that? – That is a table.
Was ist das? – Das ist eine Lampe. | What is that? – That is a lamp.
Was ist das? – Das ist ein Buch. | What is that? – That is a book.
Das ist ein Hund. Der Hund heißt Rex. | That is a dog. The dog is called Rex.
```

Note how the second sentence uses **the definite article** (*der Hund*): you introduce something new with *ein/eine*, and then, once it is known, you refer to it with *der/die/das*. English does the same ("a dog … the dog").

## 3.4 Plurals

The plural article is always **die**, whatever the gender. The plural noun itself changes in one of several ways. There are five endings and an optional **umlaut** (a → ä, o → ö, u → ü, au → äu):

| Pattern | Example | Typical for |
|---|---|---|
| **-e** (± umlaut) | der Tisch → die Tisch**e**; der Stuhl → die St**ü**hl**e**; der Hund → die Hund**e** | many masculine nouns |
| **-er** (± umlaut) | das Kind → die Kind**er**; das Buch → die B**ü**ch**er**; das Haus → die H**ä**us**er** | many neuter nouns |
| **-(e)n** | die Lampe → die Lampe**n**; die Katze → die Katze**n**; die Frau → die Frau**en** | **almost all feminine nouns** |
| **-s** | das Auto → die Auto**s**; das Handy → die Handy**s**; das Foto → die Foto**s** | words from other languages, ending in a vowel |
| **no ending** (± umlaut) | das Zimmer → die Zimmer; der Bruder → die Br**ü**der; das Fenster → die Fenster | nouns ending in -er, -el, -en |

```merke
**Plural shortcuts that cover most nouns:**
- Feminine → almost always **-n / -en** (*die Lampe → die Lampen, die Tür → die Türen*).
- Nouns ending in **-e** → add **-n** (*die Katze → die Katzen, der Junge → die Jungen*).
- Foreign words and many short words ending in a vowel → **-s** (*das Auto → die Autos*).
- Nouns ending in **-er, -el, -en** → usually **no ending**, sometimes with an umlaut (*das Zimmer → die Zimmer, der Bruder → die Brüder*).
```

This book writes the plural after the noun in the vocabulary lists: **-e**, **¨-e** (umlaut + e), **-er**, **¨-er**, **-n**, **-en**, **-s**, **-** (no change), **¨-** (umlaut only). Learn it with the noun.

Some nouns only exist in the singular (*das Wasser, das Internet*) and some only in the plural: *die Eltern* (parents), *die Leute* (people), *die Geschwister* (siblings).

## 3.5 The verb haben ("to have")

After *sein*, **haben** is the second most important verb. It is irregular in the *du* and *er/sie/es* forms (the *b* disappears):

| | haben |
|---|---|
| ich | **habe** |
| du | **hast** |
| er / sie / es | **hat** |
| wir | **haben** |
| ihr | **habt** |
| sie / Sie | **haben** |

```examples
Ich habe eine Schwester. | I have a sister.
Hast du Geschwister? | Do you have siblings?
Anna hat einen Bruder. | Anna has a brother.
Wir haben keine Kinder. | We have no children.
```

*Haben* is also used in many fixed phrases where English uses "be": **Hunger haben** (to be hungry), **Durst haben** (to be thirsty), **Zeit haben** (to have time).

## 3.6 The object: the accusative case

In *Ich habe einen Bruder* the word for "brother" is not **ein Bruder** but **einen Bruder**. That is the **accusative case**, the form used for the **direct object**: the person or thing that the action is directed at. Both *haben* and many other verbs (sehen, brauchen, kaufen, lieben) take an accusative object.

Here is the best news about the accusative: **only the masculine changes**.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| definite, nominative (subject) | der | die | das | die |
| definite, **accusative** (object) | **den** | die | das | die |
| indefinite, nominative | ein | eine | ein | – |
| indefinite, **accusative** | **einen** | eine | ein | – |

Both changes add an **-n**: *der → den, ein → einen*. Everything else stays exactly as it is.

```examples
Ich habe einen Bruder. | I have a brother. (der Bruder)
Ich habe eine Schwester. | I have a sister. (die Schwester)
Ich habe ein Kind. | I have a child. (das Kind)
Ich sehe den Hund. | I see the dog. (der Hund)
Ich sehe die Katze. | I see the cat.
Ich brauche einen Stuhl. | I need a chair.
```

```achtung
**A common beginner error: "Ich habe ein Bruder."** The brother is the object of *haben*, so the masculine article becomes **einen**. Say it aloud a few times: *ich habe einen Bruder, einen Hund, einen Tisch.*
```

## 3.7 Saying "no": kein and nicht

German has two negations, and choosing the right one is easy once you know the rule.

- **kein** negates a **noun** that has **ein/eine or no article**: *Ich habe ein Auto → Ich habe kein Auto.*
- **nicht** negates **everything else**: verbs, adjectives, places, nouns with *der/die/das* or *mein/dein*.

| | Example |
|---|---|
| ein/eine/no article → **kein** | Das ist ein Hund. → Das ist **kein** Hund. · Ich habe Zeit. → Ich habe **keine** Zeit. |
| verb or adjective → **nicht** | Ich arbeite **nicht**. · Anna ist **nicht** müde. |
| definite noun, mein/dein → **nicht** | Das ist **nicht** der Hund. · Das ist **nicht** mein Bruder. |

*kein* takes the same endings as *ein*: **kein / keine / kein**, and in the accusative masculine **keinen**. The plural is **keine**.

```examples
Das ist kein Hund. | That is not a dog.
Ich habe keine Katze. | I don't have a cat.
Ich habe keinen Bruder. | I don't have a brother.
Wir haben keine Kinder. | We have no children.
Ich bin nicht müde. | I'm not tired.
```

Another small word that helps: answering a negative question. If someone asks *Hast du keine Schwester?* ("Don't you have a sister?"), the German **doch** means "yes, I do": *Doch, ich habe eine Schwester.* And *nein* means "no, I don't". English has no one-word equivalent.

## 3.8 mein, dein: my and your

Possessive words ("my, your, his…") take the same endings as **ein / kein**: no ending for masculine and neuter, **-e** for feminine and plural, and **-en** for masculine accusative.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| my | mein Bruder | meine Schwester | mein Kind | meine Eltern |
| your (informal) | dein Bruder | deine Schwester | dein Kind | deine Eltern |
| masculine accusative | Ich sehe **meinen** Bruder. | | | |

The other possessives (sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr) follow the same pattern and are introduced in later chapters.

```examples
Das ist mein Vater. | That is my father.
Das ist meine Mutter. | That is my mother.
Meine Eltern wohnen in Hamburg. | My parents live in Hamburg.
Wie heißt dein Bruder? | What is your brother's name?
Ich sehe meinen Bruder. | I see my brother.
```

## 3.9 Describing things: groß, klein, neu, alt

You describe a noun with *sein* plus an adjective. In this position the adjective has **no ending**:

```examples
Das Haus ist groß. | The house is big.
Die Katze ist klein. | The cat is small.
Mein Handy ist neu. | My phone is new.
Der Tisch ist alt. | The table is old.
```

## 3.10 Summary

```merke
- Every noun is **capitalised** and has a gender: **der, die, das**. Plural article: **die**.
- Learn each noun **with article and plural**.
- Gender rules: **-ung/-heit/-keit/-in → die**, **-chen/-lein → das**, people follow natural sex, **compounds follow the last word**, **-e is mostly feminine**.
- **ein** (masc., neuter) · **eine** (fem.). No plural.
- Plurals: **-e, -er, -(e)n, -s, no change**, often with an umlaut. Feminine → almost always **-(e)n**.
- **haben:** habe, hast, hat, haben, habt, haben.
- **Accusative (object):** only masculine changes: **den, einen, keinen, meinen**.
- **kein** negates nouns with ein/no article; **nicht** negates verbs, adjectives and definite nouns.
- **mein / dein** take the same endings as **ein**.
```

```kultur
**Landeskunde: the family.** In German-speaking countries the typical household is small. Grandparents are *Oma* and *Opa* (or *Großmutter* and *Großvater*). A very popular way to say "my parents" is *meine Eltern*; "my siblings" is *meine Geschwister*. For a partner, *mein Freund* and *meine Freundin* mean boyfriend and girlfriend, not just "friend". For a platonic friend you often say *ein guter Freund* or *eine gute Freundin*.
```
